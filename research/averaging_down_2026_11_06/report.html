Historical risk study · November 6, 2026

When does adding to a losing position earn its risk?

The strongest finding is about exposure: fixed additions can improve the win rate while concentrating much larger losses in the failures. This study does not establish a dependable, fast-recovery trading edge from automatically adding every 5%.

The question is not whether MRCY rebounded: it did. The question is whether a repeatable rule could capture recoveries while controlling the losses when they fail. All figures below come from saved broker records or historical provider bars, with execution assumptions stated explicitly.

1. What happened in MRCY

The recorded November 4–6 sequence ended with −$9,227.46 in cash P&L after broker-reported trade charges, before financing and tax effects. That is the complete MRCY sequence, not a claim about one day’s total account P&L. The ledger contains 93 MRCY entries, including 17 with provisional timestamps. November 4 intraday ordering therefore cannot be reconstructed exactly.

From November 5 onward, the holdings reached 2,700 shares. Net cash committed to the entire idea, including earlier realized losses, peaked at $187,270.63. A $1 price move at 2,700 shares changes mark-to-market value by $2,700.

On November 6, MRCY opened at $66.93, fell to $64.3706, reached $73.91, and closed at $73.55. The next-day bounce was substantial, but it followed another low. The original $78.74 entry price was still above that day’s close; the benefit of holding depended on the accumulated lower-price purchases.

A hindsight calculation that retains the final 1,600 shares sold late November 5 and on November 6, then liquidates at November 6’s official close, changes the campaign to about $3,323.52 before the replacement exit’s trading costs and financing. This assumes the account could hold the shares, makes no prediction, and uses an exit selected after seeing the outcome.

If that adverse excursion is intolerable, the remedy is smaller exposure or a different strategy—not assuming the recovery will arrive before the account or investor gives up.

2. What the broader market history says

The broad cohort consists of liquid stocks closing at least 5% below the prior close. The initial hypothetical purchase is at the following session’s open. This is a post-drop entry study, not a backtest of the app’s morning selection model. MRCY was excluded from the broad comparisons. Events for the same ticker are separated by more than 21 sessions.

The daily panel covers 2021-11-01 through 2026-11-06; sufficient lookback and forward coverage produce entries from 2022-01-28 through 2026-10-09. Development entries precede 2025; later evaluation entries are in 2025–2026. Entry eligibility uses a $5 minimum split-adjusted price, prior 20-session average dollar volume of at least $10M, and 60 prior sessions. Full future windows are required, a selection limitation discussed below.

Among later-period events, about 29.9% touched the pre-drop close within five sessions, while 15.9% fell another 10% below the next-day entry during that period. By 20 sessions those figures were 57.2% and 40.2%. A path can do both. More time mechanically creates more opportunities to touch a recovery price and also exposes the position to additional adverse moves.

For the 20-session daily decision version, adding increased mean P&L from roughly $272 to $452, and the win rate from 54.3% to 63.8%. However, average maximum capital deployed rose from $10,000 to $19,538, and the average loss in the worst 5% of trials rose from roughly $2,976 to $8,024. This is extra expected dollars accompanied by substantially greater capital use and loss concentration; it is not a free improvement.

The matched-date SPY comparison also matters: the stocks’ average 20-session price return was 2.94%, versus 1.66% for SPY over the same weighted windows. Only about 49.9% of stocks beat SPY, and the median excess return was approximately zero. These are raw, unadjusted-for-risk comparisons, not proof of stock-selection skill.

Cost and execution sensitivity

Base assumptions are 10 bps slippage plus 1 bp fees per side, fractional shares, and complete fills at the next available minute open. These are modeling choices, not claims about your actual broker charges. At 25 bps slippage plus 1 bp fees, the quick-profit rule’s average falls to roughly $7; the rebound-confirmation rule’s average becomes about −$5. If the purchases were fully financed at a hypothetical 12% annual rate, those become approximately −$2 and −$15. Your actual borrowing rate and borrowed amount were not assumed to equal these examples.

4. The arithmetic of your proposed $10K additions

Four equal $10K purchases after successive 5% price declines occur at 100%, 95%, 90.25%, and 85.7375% of the first price. They produce an average entry about 92.4425% of the original price. Lowering the average cost does not remove the loss already incurred.

  • At the fourth fill, the position is already down $2,901, before costs.
  • From that fourth fill, price must rebound about 7.82% to reach the aggregate purchase cost, before costs.
  • At 20% below the initial price, the loss is about $5,384.
  • At 30% below the initial price, the loss is about $9,711.

For an illustrative $2K loss budget and a planned exit 20% below the first entry, the same four-entry shape would allow approximately $3,715 per purchase, or $14,859 total, before fees, slippage, and gap risk. This is sizing arithmetic, not a recommendation to use a 20% stop. No stop guarantees the assumed exit price.

A general sizing check is Σ shares_i × (entry_i − planned_exit_price) + estimated costs ≤ idea_loss_budget. Include the risk from every planned addition before the first purchase. Literal doubling of each new purchase would instead total $10K + $20K + $40K + $80K = $150K; the tested proposal uses equal additions.

5. A decision framework supported by this study

  1. Set the dollar loss budget before entering, then derive position size. Account equity, borrowed exposure, other positions, and the maximum tolerable loss are still needed to choose personal dollar limits. A position cap is useful, but it is not itself a loss cap.
  2. Treat each addition as a fresh investment decision. A lower average cost is an accounting consequence, not evidence of positive expected returns. The specific price-only rules tested here do not justify automatically adding at every 5% decline.
  3. Charge all partial exits and renewed purchases to one idea-level budget. Do not reset the loss allowance after realizing a loss or reducing the displayed average price. The simulation has no re-entry after a full exit, so repeated attempts could lose more than these results.
  4. Make the holding period explicit. The current app’s morning forecasts target the open-to-close session. Holding overnight is a separate strategy with gap risk, financing, and event exposure. The historical five- and twenty-session tests are distinct alternatives, not permission to extend a losing trade indefinitely.
  5. Choose an exit policy while flat, and assess its full payoff distribution. Quick recovery targets increase win rate but cap many gains; partial-profit and trailing exits were not a clear fix in this sample. A missed rebound does not invalidate a risk limit chosen for survivability.
  6. Use paper trading to validate the next version. Preserve a no-add baseline, predeclare the risk budget and time exit, and measure costs and fill quality. The present evidence supports exposure discipline more strongly than a particular dip-buying threshold.

Prior trading profits can inform how much account drawdown you are willing to tolerate. They provide no new information about a stock’s recovery probability. If approaching the loss limit causes the planned exit to be postponed, the limit ceases to perform its intended role.

Stop orders can fill materially beyond their trigger price; stop-limit orders can fail to fill. The minute simulation includes gap and delayed-execution overshoots—for example, its $2K-trigger variants include losses above $3K. FINRA’s stop-order explanation describes these execution limitations. A margin deficiency can also lead to broker liquidation, so an investor may not control how long a leveraged position remains open. See FINRA’s margin guidance. Concentration across several correlated positions can make the account’s total risk larger than a per-stock rule suggests; see FINRA’s concentration-risk explanation.

6. What remains uncertain

  • Survivorship and selection: the universe is based on recently selected stocks, not a complete point-in-time universe including every failed or delisted company. Requiring complete future windows can further understate failure risk. The observed frequencies are conditional on this dataset.
  • Corporate actions: the broad data are provider split-adjusted price bars. Cash dividends, tax effects, point-in-time symbol identity changes, and independently verified corporate-action records are not fully modeled. These are exploratory results, not the repository’s certified overnight-data contract.
  • Chronological holdout, not a live forward test: the later period was kept separate in reporting; it is still historical. Fifteen predeclared policies and descriptive subgroup comparisons do not eliminate multiple-comparison risk.
  • Dependence: stocks share market shocks and recur across dates. Bootstrap diagnostics cluster by ticker and entry date separately, taking the wider interval; they are not a formal multi-way coverage guarantee.
  • Portfolio feasibility: per-event profits cannot be summed into an investable portfolio return. Concurrent opportunities, capital reservations, correlated exposure, and margin constraints were not simulated as a full account.
  • Event information: earnings, guidance changes, news, fundamentals, and point-in-time news classifications are not validated predictors in this study. A falling price alone cannot distinguish temporary selling pressure from permanent impairment.
  • Actual records: 17 MRCY entries retain provisional times; full-period cash P&L is reconstructable, but the November 4 intraday path is not exact.
